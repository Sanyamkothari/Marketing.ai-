## 1. Summary

Milestone M101 makes sure a customer gets **at most one action** (or the configured contact cap) in a cycle across
the use cases, and that each use case can still measure its own effect.

1. **Arbitration engine (`engine/decide/arbitrate.py`).** `arbitrate_treat_lists` is a pure function over the
   treat lists (M98) of several scoring runs; the order of the lists is the request's use case order.
   - Hold-out members are never treated by any use case.
   - A row M92 treated at random (`explore = 1`, `treat = 1`) keeps its action, like hold-out protection. Two
     explore-treated rows of one customer are both kept only if the contact cap allows; otherwise the first by
     request order is kept and the other is recorded as dropped.
   - The rest are ranked by `priority x value` **only when every candidate of the customer carries the same kind of
     value** (all `net_value`, or all `expected_gross_value`). Otherwise they are ranked by priority alone. A tie is
     broken by the request's use case order. A candidate with no value is never given one: there is no
     `fillna(1.0)`, and incremental and gross values are never compared.
   - `contact_cap_per_customer` (default 1) and `channel_caps` (default none) hold.
   - Every row says why (`arbitration_reason`); the summary counts how the customers who lost an action were decided.
2. **No configuration ships.** `configs/decide/arbitration.example.yaml` is a labelled example that is never read.
   With no `decide/arbitration.yaml` in the config root: priority 1 per use case, cap 1 per customer, no channel caps.
3. **One campaign per use case, comparing like with like** (`create_arbitrated_campaign`,
   `engine/measurement/campaign.py`; `comparable_keys`, `engine/decide/arbitrate.py`): the population is the use
   case's own policy, and both arms (treated and held back) are cut by one rule that ignores the hold-out - the
   arbitration run again as if no one were held back, a held-back customer the policy intended competing like a
   treated one. A customer a rival wins is in neither arm. The campaign has `POST /campaigns`' terms (start = the
   run's finish, the use case's window, the hold-out requirement) and posting twice makes one set.
4. **Row-level privacy and access:** the two files are row-level artefacts (`configs/privacy.yaml`,
   `engine/privacy/layout.py`, `api/access_policy.py`) kept only in the participating runs (`runs/<id>/`), where
   retention finds them; there is no copy at the store root. Downloads are Analyst-only and audited and are served
   from a participating run's copy.
5. **Results UI:** the `Arbitration & Conflicts` card.
6. **Scale:** whole-array work; 200,000 customers take about 1 second across two use cases and 1.5 seconds across
   three with every path on (about 5 to 8 seconds for a million). The wall-clock budget tests are `slow`; a fast
   test checks that the time grows linearly.

## 7. Open Questions and Known Gaps

1. **`scripts/bench_1m.py` unreachable warning on macOS** is pre-existing and left alone.
2. **A control group of one use case's own** - closed by DEC-1311 (al)-(aq) (integrated 2026-10-09). Phase 1's per-run control (not M92's
   persistent hold-out) used to be missing from the treat list as a flag, so another use case could treat that
   customer. The treat list now carries `control_group`; arbitration treats a customer in the control group of any
   selected use case's run like a hold-out member (no other use case treats them, the row is the use case that held
   them back and `control_use_cases` names it), and `comparable_keys` and the campaign arms cut them by the same
   rule as hold-out members (item 5). A customer in another use case's M92 hold-out who is also in a control group
   keeps the hold-out's row ((aq)); an older treat list without the column gets it from the run's scores in memory,
   and its stored files are left as they were ((al)).
3. **A malformed `arbitration.yaml`** is refused (422 `ARBITRATION_CONFIG_INVALID`), not replaced by the defaults
   (second review; the partner's code logged it and ran on the defaults). Only an absent file gives the defaults.
4. **Channel capacity is allocated once, after the contact cap**: a customer whose action is capped out of a channel
   is not moved to another channel or to their next-best action (the second review proposed it as optional; it is
   left out because each use case's list names one planned channel per row and moving a customer would make an
   action nobody planned).
5. **Dilution in a campaign.** A customer kept from being contacted by *another* use case's hold-out, or by a channel
   cap, stays in both arms of the campaign (intent to treat): the measured effect is of being on the list, diluted
   by the share not contacted. The alternative (dropping them from one arm only) biases the comparison, and
   dropping them from both needs the hold-outs' scopes.
6. **`POST /decide/arbitrate` selects the latest scoring run of each use case** (the one that finished last) unless `run_ids` are given.
7. **A multi-offer campaign's population** - closed (opened at the Phase 2 gate, 2026-10-09, DEC-1311 (ab); closed by
   DEC-1311 (af)-(ak)). An arbitrated campaign of a use case with several offers used to be cut by the first offer's
   `intended_treatment`, so about 21% of the customers that use case contacts (413 of 1,945 in the Phase 2 journey)
   were in neither arm. The campaign is now cut by the offer choice's own `policy_intended`, the offer the policy
   would choose if nobody were held back. Under a total budget the budget walk is replayed with its own fit test at
   each customer's place, so the treated arm is exactly the contacted list, a customer the walk skipped is in neither
   arm, and a held-back customer is in the hold-out arm when the walk had room for their offer at their place.
   `POST /campaigns` on a single run of several offers is cut the same way. A held-back multi-offer row now carries the
   would-be offer's net value, so a customer held back and intended by both that use case and a one-offer use case
   goes to the larger value, not the first in the request. `tests/integration/decide/test_phase2_journey.py` asserts
   the arms exactly, and `tests/integration/decide/test_policy_offer.py` does so on the budgeted run. What stays: a
   customer a rival's hold-out or a channel cap kept from being contacted is in both arms (item 5).

## 8. Self-Check (§4.7 Checklist), as true of the code after the fixes

1. **Test the public entry point, with a real run's configuration.** `test_arbitration_review.py` posts to
   `/decide/arbitrate` over runs written by `write_run` and treat lists built by `build_treat_list`, and checks the
   campaigns' assignments; `test_arbitrate_real_runs.py` calls the engine on the same kind of lists. The partner's
   `test_arbitrate.py` builds its lists by hand; the review's tests do not.
2. **Compute once, pass everything down.** Row keys, customer ids, weights and value kinds are computed once for all
   lists; the winners, losers and reasons come from the same sorted arrays.
3. **Linear time at 1M rows.** Whole-array sorts, bincounts and Arrow string kernels; no loop over the rows. 200,000
   customers: about 1 second for two use cases, 1.5 seconds for three with explore, hold-outs and a channel cap
   (`test_arbitrate.py::test_arbitrate_scale_200k_rows`, `test_arbitrate_scale.py`).
4. **Nothing fabricated, nothing mislabelled.** No `fillna(1.0)`: a missing value stays missing and the row's
   `priority_score` is blank. Incremental and gross values are never compared. A non-winning row names the use
   case that held the customer back instead of marking another use case's row. Tests:
   `test_a_missing_value_is_never_replaced_by_a_made_up_one`, `test_incremental_and_gross_values_are_never_compared`,
   `test_a_hold_out_member_is_never_treated_by_any_use_case`.
