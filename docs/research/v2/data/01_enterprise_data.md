# Enterprise data: what big companies hold, how a vendor gets at it, and whether it fixes thin per-person signal

Research date: 21 October 2026. Target client: a large company (food delivery and consumer apps such as Zomato, Swiggy or Uber Eats; SaaS; banks; telecoms; insurers; large retailers). Small-shop commerce notes are kept short in section 9.

The problem we are trying to solve comes from [REAL_DATA_RESULTS.md](../../../v2/REAL_DATA_RESULTS.md): per-person learning starves because a typical shop customer has about one purchase on record, and causal lift cannot be learned from history without control groups. Access, auth, rate limits and write-back for ESPs are in [02_apis_and_write_back.md](../connectors/02_apis_and_write_back.md) and are not repeated.

## 1. The short answer

1. **Per-person depth: enterprise data fixes it where people use the product often, and does not where they do not.** A food delivery user orders about 4 times a month (Swiggy 4.1, Q2 FY26). A telecom subscriber produces usage and recharge records every day. A bank customer in India makes tens of UPI payments a month. Those populations have dense per-person histories. An insurer's policyholder touches the company about once a year, which is the same starvation we saw on shop data. Retailers sit in between.
2. **Dense behaviour is not dense response.** Even at 4 orders a month, each person receives a handful of marketing messages and responds to few. Per-person *response* learning stays thin everywhere. What improves is per-person *state* (taste, timing, value, churn risk), which is the context the decision needs.
3. **Churn labels stay sparse even where usage is dense.** Monthly telecom churn is in the low single digits (Airtel 2.6% in one quarter, Doc/news, older), and subscription DTC churn is about 6-7% a month. A churn model has dense inputs and a rare outcome. Food delivery lapse is far more frequent, so it is the better first domain.
4. **Randomised signal exists inside big companies, but it is not in the warehouse by default.** Large marketing teams run A/B tests and global holdouts (Klaviyo and Bird both document them; Braze and CleverTap not confirmed). Assignment logs live in the experimentation tool or the CRM tool, not always in the warehouse. We must ask for them by name, check how assignment was done, and keep running our own holdout. At enterprise scale a 1% holdout of 20 million users is 200,000 people, so our own control group is cheap and well powered.
5. **The deployment model is the real gate.** Big companies, especially banks and telecoms, will not export person-level PII to a vendor. The workable pattern is warehouse-native: run inside their Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks or Redshift or their VPC, read their tables, write decisions back to a table. This also meets India's data-storage and outsourcing rules more easily. It collides with our current engine, which holds about 2 KB of memory per event and was killed at 1 million customers (REAL_DATA_RESULTS failure 11). At enterprise scale features must be computed in SQL inside the warehouse, not replayed as events in our process.

## 3. What data big companies hold, by system

| System | What it holds | Per-person volume | History depth | Freshness | Powers |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Data warehouse / lakehouse** (Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks, Redshift) | Orders, payments, app events (often raw, huge), CRM extracts, campaign logs, sometimes experiment tables | See section 2 | Typically years; raw events often kept 13-25 months at least (Verify per customer) | Batch (hourly to daily) or streaming ingestion | Every decision; the main source |
| **CDP** (Segment, mParticle, Tealium, Adobe Real-Time CDP, Salesforce Data Cloud) | Unified profile with identity resolution, event stream, audiences, consent | Whatever the customer forwards in; profiles merge devices | Event history depends on plan and retention; replay may cost extra (Verify) | Real time | Identity join, consent, live triggers |
| **Product analytics** (Amplitude, Mixpanel) | Event streams with user and device IDs, cohorts, funnels, experiment exposures (Amplitude Experiment, Mixpanel Experiments) | Hundreds of events per active user per month for a busy app (Est.) | Retention by plan (often years on enterprise plans, Verify) | Near real time; warehouse sync available | Timing, product choice, engagement-based risk |
| **Experimentation platform** (Optimizely, Statsig, Eppo, in-house) | Assignment and exposure logs, metrics, test results | One row per unit per test | Test history, if retained | Real time exposure logging | Randomised signal (section 6) |
| **CRM / engagement tool** (Braze, CleverTap, MoEngage, Salesforce Marketing Cloud, Iterable) | Sends, opens, clicks, conversions, holdout flags, frequency caps, unsubscribes | A few to dozens of messages per person per month | Often 1-2 years in the tool; Braze engagement events at scale need paid Currents (see connectors note) | Real time | Who to leave alone, fatigue, past campaign lift |
| **Core banking / cards / payments** | Accounts, balances, transactions with merchant category, card scheme, channel; loans, deposits, KYC | Tens of transactions a month (UPI figure above) | Years; regulated retention | Batch end of day plus streaming for card authorisations | Spend patterns, product propensity, risk, dormancy |
| **Bank CRM** (e.g. Salesforce Financial Services Cloud, Data Cloud Financial Account Transaction model) | Parties, financial accounts and their links, applications, balances, transactions when fed from core banking | Few records per person per year for interactions | Years | Batch | Cross-sell, relationship view |
| **Telecom billing, recharge and network** | Recharge and plan data, billing, usage summaries, CDRs and data-session records, network quality, complaints, port-out requests | Daily usage records; roughly monthly recharges on prepaid | Months to years; CDRs often retained shorter for cost (Verify) | Batch to near real time | Plan fit, churn risk, win-back |

| **App event stream** (Kafka, Kinesis, Pub/Sub, Firebase) | Sessions, screen and item views, searches, carts, orders, delivery status, support chats | Sessions often daily for engaged users; order events 4 a month for food delivery | Hot stream plus warehouse archive | Real time | Timing, product choice, in-session offers |

## 4. How a vendor gets access

The goal is to read the customer's data and write decisions back without copying person-level PII into our own estate.

| Route | How it works | Fits when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Snowflake Native App** | The app is installed in the customer's Snowflake account. By default it gets no consumer data. The customer grants privileges on named tables, views and warehouses, and can review the manifest at install. The customer cannot see the provider's code or data. Apps with containers (Snowpark Container Services) run in an isolated network, must declare external endpoints in the manifest, and need Snowflake Product Security approval before a public or private listing ([consumer model](https://docs.snowflake.com/en/developer-guide/native-apps/consumer-guide-model), [security with SPCS](https://docs.snowflake.com/developer-guide/native-apps/security-na-spcs), Doc) | Customer is on Snowflake and wants "data never leaves" | Cross-cloud fulfilment limits (AWS, Azure in preview per one extract); our compute runs on their credits; a listing and review cycle |
| **Snowflake secure share / service user** | Customer shares tables to our account, or gives a key-pair service user to read | Faster pilot than a Native App | Data reaches our account; many banks refuse |
| **BigQuery sharing (formerly Analytics Hub)** | A publisher shares a dataset as a read-only linked dataset in the subscriber's project; publishers can see and remove subscribers; the subscriber needs the Subscriber role on the listing ([intro](https://docs.cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/analytics-hub-introduction), [subscribe](https://docs.cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/analytics-hub-view-subscribe-listings), Doc). For us, the customer is the publisher and our project is the subscriber. The simpler alternative is `dataViewer` on a dataset for our service account (connectors note) | Customer on Google Cloud | No copy, no storage cost for us; query cost lands on the project that runs the query |
| **Databricks Delta Sharing** | Open protocol: shares Unity Catalog tables with recipients on any platform without copying, with Databricks-to-Databricks and open-recipient modes ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/blog/2021/05/26/introducing-delta-sharing-an-open-protocol-for-secure-data-sharing.html), Doc) | Lakehouse customers, including those on other clouds | Marketplace and Apps routes were not covered by any extract (Verify) |
| **Redshift data sharing** | Cross-account read-only share with a two-way handshake; both clusters must be encrypted; consumer queries run on consumer compute; no extra charge ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.eu/redshift/latest/dg/across-account.html), Doc). AWS Data Exchange for Redshift handles commercial listings | Customer on AWS | Needs a Redshift consumer on our side; many AWS shops use S3 plus Athena or Glue instead |
| **Salesforce Data Cloud zero-copy** | Live query of Data Cloud objects such as Unified Individual from Snowflake, and the reverse, without moving data ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/in/blog/zero-copy/), Vendor) | Customer's profile lives in Data Cloud | Vendor description; check the object coverage per customer |
| **CDP destination** (Segment, mParticle, Tealium, Adobe) | The customer routes a chosen event stream and audiences to us through a destination we build or a webhook. Reported ecosystem sizes: Tealium 1,300+ vendors, Segment 700+, mParticle 300+ ([RudderStack](https://www.rudderstack.com/competitors/segment-vs-tealium/), Vendor, competitor source) | Real-time triggers, customers with no warehouse access for vendors | Only the events the customer forwards; no history unless replayed; partner review needed (connectors note) |
| **Reverse ETL for write-back** (Hightouch, Census, CDP reverse ETL) | We write a decisions table in their warehouse; their tool pushes it to the CRM, push or SMS tool | Almost all large customers | Covered in the connectors note |
| **In-VPC or customer-hosted deployment** | We ship containers into the customer's cloud account; access via role assumption and private networking | Banks, telecoms, regulated insurers | Customer carries cost and ops; slower upgrades; heaviest sales cycle |
| **Raw files (S3, GCS, SFTP)** | Customer drops extracts | Fastest pilot | PII leaves their estate; weakest security story |

The design rule that satisfies most of this at once: **decide next to the data**. Features and scores are computed inside the customer's warehouse by queries we ship, the policy runs there, and only decisions (pseudonymous ID, action, time, reason) leave through the customer's own reverse ETL or CRM. That also removes the memory ceiling.

## 6. Does enterprise data give randomised or control-group signal?

Short answer: **it can, and it is the biggest advantage of enterprise over small shops, but only if we find the right tables, check how assignment worked, and do not trust it by default.**

| Source | What it gives | Typical size | How to get it | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Experiment platform logs** (Optimizely, Statsig, Eppo, in-house) | Assignment and exposure per unit for every test run; warehouse-native versions work directly on Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks and Redshift (Optimizely marketing, [source](https://www.optimizely.com/field-notes/articles/optimizely-analytics-versus-amplitude-statsig-and-eppo/), Vendor). Which tools write exposure rows to the customer's warehouse and in what schema was not found | Many tests a year in a mature team; Verify | Ask for the exposure table and the test registry | Unit may be a session, device or restaurant, not a person. Assignment propensity may not be logged. Overlapping tests contaminate each other. App tests are mostly about product UI, not messages |
| **CRM global holdouts and campaign control groups** | A random share of users suppressed from all messages, which gives the organic baseline and the program-level lift. Klaviyo documents global holdouts (suggests three months, aimed at large bases such as 400,000+ profiles) and Bird documents holdouts with the incrementality formula (exposed rate minus holdout rate, divided by holdout rate) ([Klaviyo](https://www.klaviyo.com/product/whats-new/global-holdout-groups), [Bird](https://docs.bird.com/applications/campaigns/campaigns/concepts/holdout-groups), Doc/Vendor). Braze and CleverTap behaviour was not confirmed | Often 1-10% of the base (Est.; no benchmark found) | Ask for holdout flags and the campaign log from the CRM tool or its warehouse export | Holdouts exempt transactional messages and are often broken by other tools (push, SMS, ads) that ignore the flag |
| **Promotion and pricing experiments** (delivery fee, coupon depth, loyalty tiers, free delivery thresholds) | Randomised or geo-split price and offer exposure with order outcomes | Large for food delivery and retail (Est.) | Ask the pricing and growth teams for test records | Often run at city or restaurant level, not person level. Targeted promotions (to lapsed users) are not random |
| **Staggered rollouts, geo tests and feature flags** | Quasi-experiments with a known assignment | Common | Flag logs | Needs design-aware analysis; weaker than person-level randomisation |
| **Logged bandit or ML-targeted campaigns** | Valid only if propensities were logged | Rare (Verify) | Ask whether a propensity column exists | Without a logged propensity the data is non-random for our purposes (this is the Open Bandit lesson in REAL_DATA_RESULTS) |
| **Our own holdout, in a warehouse-native deployment** | Clean randomisation we control, with the propensity we log | 1% of 20 million is 200,000; 1% of 2 million is 20,000 | Part of onboarding | Needs customer sign-off to withhold a message from a random slice; regulated sectors may require a rule that some messages are never withheld (reminders, service notices) |

What this fixes in our own findings:

- **Calibration (failure 1).** The 10x value overestimate came from having no randomised control to anchor expected lift. A customer's past holdout or A/B logs are exactly that anchor, with far more people than Hillstrom (64,000).
- **Cold start (failure 2).** With a decade of orders and campaign sends in a warehouse, the agent can warm-start from history, if the engine reads it (it does not yet).
- **Control for "leave alone".** A never-messaged holdout gives the organic order rate per segment, which is what a "would have ordered anyway" model needs.

What it does not fix:

- Historical targeted campaigns are still biased. Most promotions go to people the marketer picked.
- Per-person response depth. Even with randomisation you learn lift per segment (city, tenure, taste cluster), not per individual. That is fine: the engine should pool across people and use per-person state as the context.
- Insurers and banks: contact rules limit how often we may message, so they will give few experiments and little response data per person.

## 7. Per-domain assessment

| Domain | Per-person state depth | Response depth | Causal signal available | Best first decision | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Food delivery / consumer apps** | High: about 4 orders a month, daily app use, cuisine, time of day, delivery area | Medium: frequent messages, many tests | High: constant A/B and promo tests, probably CRM holdouts (Verify) | Timing and offer vs no offer; lapse prevention; who not to discount | Best fit. Order frequency is falling as lighter users join, so lapse and discount-dependence are live problems |
| **Retail (large)** | Medium: a few purchases a quarter, loyalty-card history, basket data | Medium | Medium: loyalty promotions, coupon tests | Product choice, replenishment timing | Resembles Online Retail and X5 in our tests |
| **SaaS** | High in product events (instrumentation dependent), low in commercial events (renewals once a year) | Low to medium | Medium: lifecycle email and in-app tests | Onboarding nudges, expansion, churn rescue | Churn label is annual, so build on activation and usage decline |
| **Telecom** | High in usage; recharge about monthly (Verify) | Medium: SMS and app offers | Medium: retention offer tests exist; churn is 1-3% a month | Plan upgrade offers, recharge reminders, retention offers | Rare churn label; offers cost real margin; strict outreach rules |
| **Banking** | High in transactions (tens a month); low in product holdings | Low: contact limits, consent rules | Low to medium: few randomised campaigns | Product propensity, dormancy reactivation | Compliance and PII constraints dominate; decide next to the data |
| **Insurance** | Low: about one meaningful touch a year | Very low | Low | Renewal timing, lapse rescue, claim-moment service | Starvation remains; pooled models only |

## 8. What this means for Autonomy

1. **Adopt a warehouse-native deployment as the default enterprise path** (Snowflake Native App or in-VPC, with a BigQuery and Databricks equivalent). It answers PII, residency and the vendor review in one move, and it removes the in-memory ceiling.
2. **Push feature computation into SQL.** Per-person state (recency, frequency, value, last order, cuisine or category mix, discount share, last-contact time, consent) should be materialised as one row per person per day. The agent reads those rows, not raw events. At 4 orders a month for 20 million users the raw stream is billions of events a year.
3. **Make the intake list explicit.** For each customer ask for: orders and payments; app events; consent and do-not-contact; CRM sends, clicks and holdout flags; experiment registry and assignment tables; promotion and pricing test records; cost or margin per order; churn or lapse definition.
4. **Treat the first 4-8 weeks as measurement.** Run our own randomised holdout in parallel with the customer's existing rules, log the propensity, and calibrate before the agent makes real decisions (the Shadow day pattern).
5. **Prefer food delivery and consumer app clients first.** They have the density and the experiment culture. Banks and insurers need a compliance-heavy pilot, and the data will not fix their per-person depth.
6. **Fix the engine items this implies:** history reaching the agent, features reaching the bidder, calibration from a control group, and a cancel or lapse event (REAL_DATA_RESULTS failures 1, 2, 5, 6).
